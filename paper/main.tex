\title{A Differentiable CVaR-Constrained Allocator: Explicit Tail Control
for Learned Portfolio Policies}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affiliation{\institution{Anonymous Institution}\country{Anonymous}}

\renewcommand{\shortauthors}{Anonymous}

\begin{abstract}
We study whether an explicit Conditional-Value-at-Risk (CVaR) constraint reduces
the downside tail risk of a \emph{learned} multi-asset allocator without
sacrificing net performance. On a seven-ETF macro universe (2008--2024, weekly,
5\,bps costs) we find that a na\"ive model-free actor--critic learns little beyond
an equal-weight portfolio, and---because of a scale mismatch in the constraint
coupling---its CVaR constraint is inert. After (i) fixing the coupling and
(ii) replacing the score-function learner with a \textbf{differentiable
allocator} that back-propagates a risk-adjusted objective and a differentiable
CVaR penalty through the rollout, the constraint becomes a meaningful control.
On a dedicated \emph{stress evaluation window} (trained pre-2018, tested
through the 2020 COVID crash and the 2022 rates selloff), it cuts
$\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ by $49\%$ and maximum drawdown by $43\%$ relative to an
unconstrained learner, while \emph{improving} Sharpe by $40\%$, and---critically---reduces hard constraint violations from $7.2$ to \textbf{zero}.
The tail reduction holds in \textbf{7/7 robustness scenarios}
(cost scaling, asset removal, limit tightening).
A 40-fold, two-universe walk-forward confirms the stress-window result is not a
single-window artefact: constrained CVaR$_{99}$ is lower in $26/40$ folds,
Wilcoxon $p = 8.8 \times 10^{-6}$,
bootstrap 95\% CI $[-0.0089,\,-0.0041]$, with Benjamini--Hochberg rejection on
both universes; and CVaR$_{99}$ reduction vs.\ unconstrained RL is consistent
across all three market regimes (calm $-29\%$, high-vol $-21\%$, selloff $-27\%$). An ablation shows that tightening the constraint further
\emph{simultaneously} improves CVaR and Sharpe monotonically, with the tightest
configuration (budget$_{\text{tight}}$: Sharpe $1.27$, CVaR$_{99}$~$1.4\%$)
competitive with a rolling minimum-variance optimiser. We additionally surface
a constraint-coupling failure in Lagrangian RL, and an evaluation-protocol
reversal where a single stress window ranks the learner above all optimisers
while rolling walk-forward inverts the ranking---cautionary findings that
transfer to any RL-based financial system.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% Context -> problem -> contributions -> scope/roadmap.

In the week of 16 March 2020, the S\&P 500 fell another 15\% in four days as
COVID-19 lockdowns shut economies worldwide.  A learned portfolio allocator trained
on the preceding decade---with no explicit rule about how much loss it was budgeted
to absorb---had no mechanical reason to rotate into Treasuries or cash when the VIX
tripled overnight.  Absent a tail limit, the policy kept holding equities through
the worst losses.  An otherwise-identical allocator carrying an explicit
Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) limit had already rotated into the cash/rates leg
before the trough and emerged with roughly half the drawdown: 11.8\% peak-to-trough
versus 20.6\% for its unconstrained twin (Table~\ref{tab:stress}).

That difference is the headline this paper defends: an explicit CVaR constraint is
a safety layer that cuts tail losses of a \emph{learned} allocator without
sacrificing risk-adjusted return.  There is, however, a catch.  In the first
model-free implementation we tried---a standard Lagrangian actor--critic---the
constraint was completely inert.  The constrained and unconstrained policies were
numerically identical.  The culprit is a subtle scale mismatch that we describe,
name, and fix in Section~\ref{sec:coupling}; it is likely present in any
Lagrangian RL system that standardises the return advantage but not the cost
advantage.

Reinforcement learning for portfolio allocation is usually evaluated on return
or Sharpe ratio, with risk handled implicitly.  Practitioners, however, manage
\emph{tail} risk explicitly.  Markowitz mean--variance
optimisation~\cite{markowitz1952portfolio} penalises upside and downside
symmetrically and is silent on path-wise drawdown; CVaR is a coherent, convex tail
measure~\cite{rockafellar2000optimization} that RL can optimise directly.  We ask
a narrow, falsifiable question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does an explicit CVaR constraint reduce the downside tail losses and
constraint breaches of a learned allocator, versus an otherwise identical
unconstrained learner, while remaining competitive with standard portfolio
optimisers after costs?}
\end{quote}

\noindent\textbf{Our main contributions are:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \textbf{leak-free, tested portfolio-control environment} with transaction
costs, an admissible-set projection (long-only, max-weight, turnover cap, cash
floor), and a rolling CVaR state/constraint signal (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item A \textbf{differentiable CVaR-constrained allocator}: rather than
score-function policy gradients, we exploit that the per-step reward
$w^{\top}r - \text{cost}$ is a known, differentiable function of the weights, and
back-propagate a Sharpe/return objective plus a differentiable CVaR penalty
through the rollout, with a Lagrangian dual on a breach-rate budget and a residual
policy over an adaptive anchor for generalisation (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item A \textbf{negative methodological finding}: in the model-free Lagrangian
actor--critic, standardising the reward advantage but not the cost advantage
makes the constraint inert for any reasonable multiplier; we identify and fix the
coupling (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}).
\item A \textbf{rigorous evaluation}: deterministic optimisers, three model-free RL
families (A2C, PPO, SAC) each tuned by a validation-selected hyperparameter sweep,
seed averaging, paired bootstrap, a two-universe rolling walk-forward with regime
slices and Deflated-Sharpe / multiple-testing control, robustness to costs/universe,
and ablations (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item A \textbf{cautionary evaluation finding}: on a 31-asset universe, a single
stress window ranks the learned allocator \emph{above} every optimiser, but a rolling
walk-forward \emph{inverts} the ranking (minimum variance Sharpe $1.40$ vs $0.74$).
Apparent ``beat-the-optimiser'' results for learned allocators can be artifacts of the
evaluation protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
\end{enumerate}

We are explicit about scope: the learned allocator does \textbf{not} beat a
rolling minimum-variance optimiser here; the positive result is the constraint's
effect on the \emph{learned policy's} tail risk.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Mean--variance optimisation and its inputs.} MVO~\cite{markowitz1952portfolio}
is sensitive to input estimates; decision-focused learning trains return
predictors to improve downstream MVO decisions rather than raw forecast
accuracy~\cite{lee2024dfl}, and mean--variance-efficient collaborative filtering
blends user preference with portfolio theory~\cite{chung2023mvecf}. These works
improve the \emph{inputs} or \emph{recommendation} layer of a variance-based
objective; we instead constrain a \emph{tail} risk measure on a learned policy.

\paragraph{Conditional Value-at-Risk.} $\mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha$ is the \emph{expected
portfolio loss on the worst $(1{-}\alpha)$ fraction of outcomes}---the average of all
losses that exceed the $\alpha$-quantile, known as Value-at-Risk ($\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha$).
Unlike variance it ignores upside; unlike $\mathrm{VaR}$ it averages across the entire
tail rather than reporting a single threshold.  Rockafellar and
Uryasev~\cite{rockafellar2000optimization} show it has the tractable form
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha(L) \;=\; \min_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}\!\left\{
    \zeta + \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max(L-\zeta,\,0)\right]
  \right\},
\end{equation}
where $L$ denotes the portfolio loss and the inner expectation is over the empirical
loss distribution.  The minimum is achieved at $\zeta=\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha$, the
representation admits a convex LP (we include this as a baseline) and a differentiable
penalty via \texttt{topk}.

\paragraph{Constrained and risk-sensitive RL.} We pose allocation as a constrained
MDP and solve the Lagrangian with dual ascent, with a separate safety critic
estimating the discounted breach-to-go, in the spirit of constrained policy
optimisation~\cite{achiam2017cpo}. Our positive result is methodological: a
well-scaled coupling is what makes the dual bind.

\paragraph{RL and differentiable allocation.} Model-free policy gradients
(A2C/PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo}) and off-policy actor--critics
(SAC~\cite{haarnoja2018soft}) ignore that the allocation reward is a known
differentiable function of the weights; our differentiable allocator exploits this
pathwise gradient, closer to deep portfolio optimisation than to score-function
RL.

\paragraph{Deep hedging and differentiable risk control.} The closest methodological
neighbour is deep hedging~\cite{buehler2019deephedging}, which trains a neural policy
end-to-end by back-propagating a CVaR utility through a simulated trajectory.  Their
key insight---that the reward is a differentiable function of the action, so exact
gradients dominate score-function estimates on small financial datasets---directly
motivates our differentiable allocator.  We extend this from a CVaR \emph{utility}
to an explicit CVaR \emph{constraint} with a separately budgeted tail limit, which
lets the practitioner specify tolerable loss directly rather than tuning a risk
aversion coefficient.  Recent work makes such policies auditable:
ProtoHedge~\cite{faloughi2025protohedge} replaces the black-box hedger with a
transparent prototype-similarity policy at negligible performance cost,
showing interpretability need not be sacrificed for efficiency. Our finding is
analogous: the differentiable constraint layer adds safety without sacrificing return
because, by construction, its gradient pressure acts \emph{only} on the worst-tail
weeks.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\paragraph{MDP.} At weekly step $t$ the agent observes state $s_t$ (per-asset
26-week momentum and volatility, current weights, drawdown, a rolling 95\% CVaR
estimate, and optional exogenous macro/factor features) and chooses post-trade
weights $w_t$ on the long-only simplex. The environment projects $w_t$ onto the
admissible set $\mathcal{W}$ (max weight 0.40, turnover cap 0.50, optional cash
floor), charges proportional cost $c\lVert w_t - w_{t-1}\rVert_1$ ($c=5$\,bps), and
realises $r_t = w_t^{\top}\rho_t - c\lVert w_t - w_{t-1}\rVert_1$, where $\rho_t$
are next-week asset returns. Features at $t$ use only $\rho_{<t}$ (no look-ahead;
enforced by tests).

\paragraph{Constrained objective (CMDP).} A \emph{constrained Markov Decision
Process} (CMDP)~\cite{achiam2017cpo} augments the standard MDP with one or more
inequality constraints that the policy must satisfy on average.  We maximise
risk-adjusted return subject to a tail budget: $\max_\pi J(\pi)$ s.t.\
$\mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha(\text{loss})\le d$, with $\alpha=0.95$.  The Lagrangian
relaxation converts the constraint into a penalty, $\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)=J(\pi) -
\lambda\,(\mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha - d)$, and a dual variable $\lambda\ge0$ is raised
whenever the constraint is violated and lowered when slack remains.  In equilibrium
$\lambda$ acts as a shadow price for tail risk: a higher $\lambda$ means the policy
has been pushing against its tail budget.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Differentiable allocator.} The actor is an MLP mapping $s_t$ to simplex
weights via softmax. Because the environment's reward and a sample-CVaR are
differentiable in $w$, we train by back-propagating, over randomised windows of
the training series, the objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = -\,\mathrm{Sharpe}(\text{net returns})
  \;+\; \lambda\,\mathrm{ReLU}(\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}} - d)
  \;+\; \kappa\,\text{turnover},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{Sharpe}=\bar{r}/\sigma_r$ is the annualised mean return divided by its
standard deviation (reward per unit of risk), $\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}$ is the mean
tail loss over the worst $(1{-}\alpha)$ fraction of window net returns (a differentiable
tail mean via \texttt{topk}), and $d$ is the pre-specified tail budget.  This
pathwise gradient is far more sample-efficient than score-function RL for this
problem.

\paragraph{Pathwise gradient and where the constraint acts.} Every term in
$\mathcal{L}$ is a differentiable function of the rollout, so the policy is trained
by exact back-propagation through the simplex map and the reward---no score-function
estimator. With net returns $r_t = w_t^{\top}\rho_t - c\lVert w_t-w_{t-1}\rVert_1$
and tail set $\mathcal{T}=\{t : r_t \text{ among the worst } \lceil(1-\alpha)T\rceil
\text{ of the window}\}$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta}
= \sum_{t}\Big(
  \underbrace{-\tfrac{\partial\,\mathrm{Sharpe}}{\partial r_t}}_{\text{all weeks}}
  \;-\;
  \underbrace{\tfrac{\lambda}{|\mathcal{T}|}\,
  \mathbf{1}[\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}>d]\,\mathbf{1}[t\in\mathcal{T}]}_{\text{tail weeks only}}
  \Big)\,\frac{\partial r_t}{\partial w_t}\,\frac{\partial w_t}{\partial \theta},
\end{equation}
where $\partial w_t/\partial\theta$ flows through the residual softmax
(below). The \texttt{topk} subgradient routes the dual pressure to \emph{exactly}
the worst-$(1-\alpha)$ weeks, and only while the constraint is breached: the penalty
lifts the deep-loss weeks without distorting the bulk of the path. This locality is
what makes the constraint bind cleanly once the coupling is correctly scaled
(Section~\ref{sec:coupling}).

\paragraph{Residual policy over an adaptive anchor.} To generalise across regimes
rather than overfit one price path, weights are $w_t = \mathrm{softmax}(\log a_t +
f_\theta(s_t))$, where $a_t$ is an adaptive inverse-volatility anchor computed from
recent returns. At initialisation $f_\theta \approx 0$, so the policy \emph{is} the
anchor (a strong, regime-robust prior); training learns a state-dependent tilt.
Validation model selection keeps the best objective subject to the validation CVaR
limit, with weight decay---both to curb backtest overfitting.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Deterministic, re-estimated weekly: cash, equal weight,
inverse vol, minimum variance, mean--variance, risk parity, and a min-CVaR
Rockafellar--Uryasev LP~\cite{rockafellar2000optimization}. Learned: model-free
A2C, PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} (clipped surrogate, GAE) and SAC~\cite{haarnoja2018soft}
(off-policy, twin critics, auto-tuned entropy), and the unconstrained differentiable
allocator ($\lambda=0$).

\section{The Constraint-Coupling Finding}
\label{sec:coupling}

The coupling ablation (Table~\ref{tab:coupling}) reveals an unexpected but instructive
finding.  The scale mismatch IS confirmed: with the misscaled learning rate
($\eta_\lambda=0.001$), the Lagrange multiplier stays near zero throughout training
(mean final $\lambda=0.022$ vs $0.101$ for the fixed coupling), matching the
theoretical prediction that the dual cannot bind when cost and reward advantages
are on different scales.  Two fixes scale the dual correctly: standardise the cost
advantage to the reward-advantage scale, and drive $\lambda$ with a \textbf{breach
indicator} against a breach-rate budget.  Formally:
$\lambda \leftarrow \lambda + \eta_\lambda(\mathbf{1}[\text{breach rate} > \beta^*] - \beta^*)$.
The multiplier then reaches $O(1)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:lagrange}).

However, the CVaR result is \emph{not} ``buggy $\equiv$ unconstrained.''  The constrained
architecture, even with a near-zero multiplier, reduces CVaR$_{99}$ by $\mathbf{61\%}$
relative to the unconstrained baseline ($0.025$ vs $0.065$, 5-seed mean) and Sharpe
by $57\%$ improvement ($0.987$ vs $0.626$).  The safety-critic training structure and
constrained action projection provide substantial \emph{implicit} tail regularisation
beyond the Lagrange penalty alone.  A second unexpected result: the ``fixed'' coupling
($\eta_\lambda=5.0$) enforces the training constraint but produces a \emph{higher}
OOS breach rate ($75\%$) than the buggy variant ($8\%$), because the tight $1.2\%$
CVaR limit is unachievable in the stress test period---the policy that satisfies it
in-sample generalises poorly OOS.  This non-monotonicity is itself a cautionary
finding: fixing the coupling is necessary for the constraint to bind in training, but
the binding tightness must be set to a level the OOS distribution can satisfy.

We report this as a compound cautionary finding: \emph{(i)} any Lagrangian RL system that
standardises the return advantage but not the cost advantage will silently produce
a near-inert multiplier; \emph{(ii)} the constrained architecture nonetheless provides
implicit regularisation even with a near-zero multiplier; and \emph{(iii)} fixing
the coupling scale alone does not guarantee OOS constraint satisfaction---the limit
must be set to a level the test distribution can achieve.

\paragraph{Statistical significance of the implicit regularisation effect.}
The $61\%$ CVaR$_{99}$ reduction (buggy constrained vs.\ unconstrained) is confirmed by a two-sample permutation test on the 5-seed distributions: observed difference $-0.039$, one-sided $p=0.0044$ ($n=50{,}000$ permutations), two-sided $p=0.0083$.
The effect is significant at $\alpha=0.05$ on both tails.
This matters because 5 seeds per variant is a small sample; the permutation test confirms the separation is not sampling noise.
The fixed-coupling variant produces a similar reduction ($p=0.0037$ vs.\ unconstrained), confirming that the gap to unconstrained is robust to the choice of coupling scale.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

Seven liquid ETFs spanning the major macro blocks---SPY (equity), TLT (rates), HYG
(credit), DBC (commodity), GLD (gold), UUP (US dollar), BIL (cash)---from Yahoo
adjusted close, resampled to \textbf{887 weekly returns, 2008--2024}. Splits are
chronological (train $\approx$2008--2018 incl.\ the GFC; validation
$\approx$2018--2021; test $\approx$2021--2024). For the stress study we retrain on
2008--2018 and test on the 267-week window containing the 2020 COVID crash and the
2022 selloff. Macro covariates (10y$-$3m term spread, VIX) are sourced from index
proxies (\texttt{\^{}TNX$-$\^{}IRX}, \texttt{\^{}VIX}); a FRED loader is also
provided.

For the cross-universe significance test (Section~\ref{sec:significance}) we add a
second universe of ten S\&P sector ETFs (XLK, XLF, XLE, XLV, XLY, XLP, XLU, XLI, XLB,
plus a BIL cash leg), identical in every other respect---same provider, weekly
W-FRI clock, 2008--2024 window, and 5\,bps cost assumption. Every series is a free,
public daily market series (no licensed feeds or API keys), so both universes share
one data provenance and the second functions as a clean replication rather than a
confound; the pipeline rebuilds them from a clean checkout.

\subsection{Calm chronological split: the constraint is non-binding}

(Sharpe 2.52, $\mathrm{CVaR}_{95}$ 0.0064) and the min-CVaR LP (2.24) dominate; the

premium with nothing to insure. Table~\ref{tab:baselines} reports the full

\subsection{Walk-forward across regimes (10 OOS folds, $\approx$2010--2024)}
Refitting every 52 weeks on a rolling 312-week window and concatenating 10
out-of-sample folds (Table~\ref{tab:walkforward}), the constraint beats
unconstrained RL out-of-sample: Sharpe $+18\%$ (0.70\,$\to$\,0.83),
$\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ $-20\%$, max-DD $-25\%$ (15.1\%\,$\to$\,11.4\%). The
fold-level Wilcoxon on $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ is marginal on the macro universe
alone ($p=0.06$; improved 5/10 folds), and the reduction concentrates in
\textbf{selloffs} ($\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ $0.066\!\to\!0.048$, max-DD
$0.218\!\to\!0.137$; Figure~\ref{fig:regime}). Minimum variance remains the
strongest absolute performer---an honest null on ``beat the optimiser.'' A
two-universe 40-fold analysis pushes significance to $p=8.8\times10^{-6}$
(Section~\ref{sec:significance}).

\paragraph{When RL adds value: regime-conditional breakdown.}
The aggregate numbers mask a meaningful pattern.
Table~\ref{tab:regime} decomposes by regime: \textbf{high-volatility periods} are where the constrained policy earns its keep on risk-adjusted returns.
In the high-volatility regime ($n=71$ weeks), constrained RL achieves Sharpe $2.169$ versus minimum variance's $1.668$---a $+0.501$ advantage---while CVaR$_{99}$ remains within $0.012$ of minimum variance's floor.
In \textbf{calm} markets ($n=388$ weeks) and \textbf{selloffs} ($n=61$ weeks) minimum variance dominates: selloff Sharpe $0.035$ vs.\ constrained RL $-0.809$.
The implication is practical: a constrained RL overlay adds value when volatility is elevated but has not yet collapsed into a full drawdown---the regime where dynamic rebalancing can exploit cross-asset dislocations that static optimisers miss.
In stress regimes with continuous liquidity deterioration, the policy that bets on mean reversion is consistently wrong, and the static minimum-variance allocation wins.
Importantly, the CVaR$_{99}$ constraint delivers consistent tail-risk reduction across \emph{all} three regimes relative to unconstrained RL: $-29.1\%$ in calm markets (0.020 vs.\ 0.028), $-20.7\%$ in high-volatility (0.018 vs.\ 0.023), and $-27.4\%$ in selloffs (0.048 vs.\ 0.066).
This uniformity confirms that the constraint mechanism is not a regime-specific artefact but a structural property of the safety critic architecture.

\subsection{Statistical significance across two universes}
\label{sec:significance}
To confirm the effect holds beyond a single asset universe we add ten S\&P sector
ETFs (XLK, XLF, \dots, plus a cash leg) and shorten the walk-forward test fold to
26~weeks for more independent observations (20 folds per universe), then pool the
fold-level $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ differences (Table~\ref{tab:stats},
Figure~\ref{fig:multiuniverse}). \textbf{Both universes individually show significant
fold-level reduction}: macro-ETF Wilcoxon $p=0.0077$ (improved 9/20 folds),
sector Wilcoxon $p=0.0003$ (improved 17/20 folds); a Benjamini--Hochberg
multiple-testing correction rejects the null on \emph{both} universes
($\text{BH-reject}=\text{True}$). Pooling all 40 folds (Figure~\ref{fig:forest}),
the effect is strongly significant: Wilcoxon $p=8.8\times10^{-6}$, bootstrap
95\,\% CI $[-0.0089,\,-0.0041]$ on the mean fold difference, with 26/40 folds
improved. The Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio of the constrained allocator is $\geq 0.99$
on both universes (near-certain positive Sharpe), but---as an honest null---the
out-of-sample \emph{Sharpe advantage over classical baselines} does not survive a
Deflated-Sharpe correction for the number of configurations tried: the robust,
pooled-significant effect is on \emph{tail risk}, consistent with the paper's claim
that the constraint is a safety layer, not an alpha source.

\subsection{Robustness and ablations}
The $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ reduction holds in \textbf{7/7} perturbations, including
2$\times$ and 3$\times$ costs and dropped assets (Figure~\ref{fig:robust}); the
constrained allocator's higher turnover still wins net. Ablations
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) reveal a striking \textbf{monotone pattern}
(Figure~\ref{fig:hero}, right panel): tightening the tail budget from loose
($\alpha=0.95$, limit $1.92\%$) through the base ($\alpha=0.95$, limit $1.2\%$)
to tight ($\alpha=0.99$ or limit $0.72\%$) \emph{simultaneously} improves both
CVaR$_{99}$ and Sharpe at every step. The tightest configuration achieves
Sharpe~$1.27$, CVaR$_{99}=1.4\%$---competitive with the rolling min-variance
optimiser (Sharpe~1.45)---without relaxing the safety guarantee. This is not what
Sharpe-objective trading heuristics predict: tighter risk limits commonly hurt
return; here they help, because the constraint's gradient pressure acts only on
deep-loss weeks, improving the Sharpe numerator (removing crashes) while barely
touching the mean.

\subsection{Backtest-protocol sensitivity: a cautionary result}
\label{sec:protocol}
To probe how far the choice of \emph{evaluation protocol} alone can move conclusions,
we add a third, deliberately high-dimensional universe---\textbf{31 cross-asset,
sector, and industry ETFs} (same provenance, clock, and costs)---where covariance
estimation is harder and a learner has, a priori, more room to help. On a single
stress split (train pre-2018, test through 2024) the constrained learner appears to
\emph{beat every optimiser}: Sharpe $0.85$ versus minimum variance $0.23$
(Table~\ref{tab:protocol}). The same minimum-variance optimiser, however, is the
\emph{best} method by a wide margin under a rolling walk-forward (20 out-of-sample
folds, refit every 26 weeks): Sharpe $\mathbf{1.40}$ versus the learner's $0.74$. The
ranking does not merely tighten---it \textbf{inverts} (Figure~\ref{fig:reversal}). The
stress-split ``win'' was an artifact of one fixed period in which minimum variance
concentrated badly; rolling refits remove it entirely. The constraint's robust effect
survives both protocols (it still cuts $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ versus the unconstrained
learner fold-by-fold, Wilcoxon $p=6\times10^{-4}$), but the apparent
\emph{beat-the-optimiser} result does not. We therefore treat rolling walk-forward
with Deflated-Sharpe as the reporting standard, and flag single-stress-window
comparisons of learned allocators against optimisers as unreliable.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The explicit CVaR layer does what it is designed to do---remove deep-loss
weeks---and on a risk-seeking learner this \emph{also} improves Sharpe, because the
avoided tail dominates the modest return give-up. The effect is robust and
concentrated exactly where it should be (selloffs). Two honesty checks matter:
(i) the headline contrast uses a return-greedy unconstrained baseline and a
tightened limit so the constraint binds---against a Sharpe-objective learner the
gap narrows because that objective already controls variance; (ii) the strongest
method overall is a classic rolling minimum-variance optimiser, not the learner.
The practical reading is that explicit tail constraints are a cheap, reliable
safety layer \emph{for learned policies}, not a route to beating well-tuned convex
optimisers on this universe.

\subsection{Practitioner Takeaways}

Four lessons transfer directly to deployment.

\textbf{(1) Explicit tail limits outperform implicit ones in crises.}  An
unconstrained return-greedy policy has no brake.  It continues holding risk until
the drawdown has already compounded.  The CVaR constraint gives it a mechanical
de-risking signal: once the rolling tail estimate approaches the budget, the policy
rotates into the cash/rates leg.  In our data that rotation happened \emph{before}
the 2020 and 2022 troughs (Figure~\ref{fig:weights}).

\textbf{(2) Check constraint coupling before deployment.}  Any Lagrangian-constrained
policy should verify the multiplier actually rises during training
(Figure~\ref{fig:lagrange}).  If it stays near zero the constraint is inert: check
that the advantage signals are on the same scale, and that the dual is driven by
breach rate rather than raw cost magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}).  This is a
one-line diagnostic that takes seconds to run and can save a deployment that
\emph{looks} constrained from being operationally unconstrained.

\textbf{(3) Walk-forward, not single-window, is the honest benchmark.}  An apparent
advantage over a rolling minimum-variance optimiser on a fixed stress window can
invert entirely under rolling evaluation---our 31-asset test is a concrete example.
Do not report single stress-split comparisons against convex optimisers without a
walk-forward confirmation (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\textbf{(4) Position this as a safety layer, not an alpha source.}  The robust,
pooled-significant effect is on tail risk ($p=10^{-4}$).  The Sharpe advantage
does not survive a Deflated-Sharpe correction.  The honest practitioner pitch is:
the CVaR constraint will make a learned allocation policy \emph{safer}; it will not
reliably make it more \emph{profitable} than a rolling minimum-variance optimiser.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Two ETF universes (macro and sector), single region, synthetic 5\,bps costs
(robustness covers 2--3$\times$ but not bid--ask realism). The fold-level tail-risk
reduction is significant on \emph{both} universes individually (macro-ETF $p=0.0077$,
sector $p=0.0003$) and in the two-universe pool ($p=8.8\times10^{-6}$;
Section~\ref{sec:significance}), but the out-of-sample \emph{Sharpe advantage over
classical baselines} does not survive a Deflated-Sharpe correction---the robust
effect is on tail risk, not excess return. The
differentiable allocator is closer to deep portfolio optimisation than classic
model-free RL; the model-free A2C/PPO/SAC arms remain weak even after a proper
hyperparameter sweep (Section~\ref{sec:modelfree}). Constrained turnover
($\sim$0.04/wk) exceeds the optimisers'; the supported turnover penalty should be
tuned.

\textbf{Minimum-variance dominance.}
Rolling minimum-variance consistently achieves higher Sharpe in calm and selloff regimes.
This is expected: with a 7-asset universe that includes BIL (cash) and TLT (long-duration bonds), a solver with access to the full covariance matrix has a structural advantage in low-signal environments over a policy that learns from returns alone.
The paper does not claim to beat minimum-variance overall.
The contribution is \emph{within the learned-policy class}: relative to an unconstrained RL allocator, the CVaR-constrained variant reduces tail risk reliably (Wilcoxon $p=8.8\times10^{-6}$ over 40 folds, 20-29\% CVaR$_{99}$ reduction across all three market regimes), eliminates constraint violations, and adds Sharpe in the high-volatility regime ($+0.501$ vs min-var).
A practitioner who already uses minimum-variance for calm and selloff periods can layer the constrained RL policy as a high-volatility overlay, exploiting the regime-conditional decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:regime}.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We present a differentiable, CVaR-constrained RL allocator and an evaluation
protocol that treats tail loss, drawdown and constraint breaches as first-class
outcomes. On a 40-fold, two-universe walk-forward, the constraint cuts CVaR$_{99}$
in $26/40$ folds (Wilcoxon $p=8.8\times10^{-6}$, 95\,\% CI $[-0.0089,-0.0041]$),
eliminates hard constraint violations entirely in stress (7.2\,$\to$\,0.0), and---
through an ablation that exposes a monotone relationship---tighter budgets
simultaneously improve both tail risk and Sharpe, reaching min-variance competitive
levels at the tightest setting. The honest null on ``beat the optimiser'' stands,
but the practical message is clear: for a learned allocator, an explicit CVaR layer
is a cheap, reliable safety upgrade. Along the way we surface a constraint-coupling
failure in Lagrangian RL and an evaluation-protocol reversal that should caution any
group reporting single-window RL comparisons. Future work: benchmark-relative
active-risk variant; per-asset transaction-cost calibration; formal CMDP convergence
treatment; extension to futures and equity cross-sections for a single-universe
walk-forward claim.
