\begin{abstract}
Two failure modes can leave Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)-constrained financial
reinforcement learning (RL) unenforced without any visible symptom. The first is a
\textbf{Lagrangian constraint-coupling failure}, wherein a scale mismatch between the reward
and constraint channels (an unstandardised cost advantage, or a dual step mis-scaled to the
CVaR excess) leaves the dual permanently inert, so that the CVaR budget is nominal rather than
enforced. The second is an \textbf{evaluation-protocol reversal}: on a 31-asset universe, a
single stress window ranks a learned allocator above every classical optimiser, while a
20-fold rolling walk-forward inverts that ranking. ``Beat-the-optimiser'' claims can
therefore be protocol artefacts, although the constraint's tail reduction survives both
protocols (Wilcoxon $p=6\times10^{-4}$). We name,
diagnose, and resolve both failures on a
CVaR-constrained portfolio allocator (seven-ETF macro universe, 2008--2024, weekly, 5\,bps
costs, 40-fold walk-forward, pooled Wilcoxon $p=8.8\times10^{-6}$, or $3\times10^{-4}$ with
folds clustered by calendar, Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) and Deflated-Sharpe controlled). Scaling
the dual correctly makes it bind in training, yet the budget can still be breached out of
sample ($71$--$78\%$ across four validation-calibrated rules), which makes budget feasibility
a second, separate knob. The remedy is a decision-time projection that estimates forward CVaR
from trailing statistics and shrinks any non-compliant proposal toward a trailing
minimum-variance anchor. At matched dual settings it reduces out-of-sample breach from
$75.1\%$ to $7.4\%$, matching the classical optimisers' own $7.9\%$ compliance rate, at
Sharpe $0.85$--$0.99$ across filtered arms against $0.90$ for rolling minimum variance. Our claim is tail-risk control rather than alpha, and it holds under
multiplicity control. The practical checklist is to put the reward and constraint channels on
a common scale, verify that the multiplier moves off zero, treat budget feasibility as a
separate knob, and enforce compliance at decision time.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% Context -> problem -> contributions -> scope/roadmap.

In the week of 16 March 2020, the S\&P 500 fell another 15\% over five sessions as
COVID-19 lockdowns shut economies worldwide. A learned portfolio allocator trained
on the preceding decade, with no explicit rule about how much loss it was budgeted
to absorb, had no mechanical reason to rotate into Treasuries or cash when the VIX
closed at a record 82.69. Absent a tail limit, the policy kept holding equities through
the worst losses. An otherwise-identical allocator carrying an explicit
Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) limit had already rotated into the cash/rates leg
before the trough and emerged with roughly half the drawdown (11.8\% peak-to-trough
versus 20.6\% for its unconstrained twin, Table~\ref{tab:stress}).

This difference is a result, although not the main finding of the paper. An explicit CVaR
constraint is a safety layer that cuts the tail losses of a \emph{learned} allocator without
sacrificing risk-adjusted return. In the first model-free implementation we tried, a
standard Lagrangian actor--critic, the \emph{dual channel was inert}: the multiplier stayed
near zero, so the mechanism meant to enforce the constraint exerted no tail pressure.
Section~\ref{sec:coupling} traces this to a scale mismatch likely present in any Lagrangian
RL system that standardises the return advantage but not the cost advantage. The constrained
\emph{pipeline} still regularises the tail through CVaR-feasible checkpoint selection and the
admissible-set projection, even when the multiplier is inert.

Reinforcement learning for portfolio allocation is usually evaluated on return
or Sharpe ratio, with risk handled implicitly. Practitioners, however, manage
\emph{tail} risk explicitly. Markowitz mean--variance
optimisation~\cite{markowitz1952portfolio} penalises upside and downside
symmetrically and is silent on path-wise drawdown. CVaR is a coherent, convex tail
measure~\cite{rockafellar2000optimization} that RL can optimise directly. We
therefore ask a narrow, falsifiable question.

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does an explicit CVaR constraint reduce the downside tail losses and
constraint breaches of a learned allocator, versus an otherwise identical
unconstrained learner, while remaining competitive with standard portfolio
optimisers after costs?}
\end{quote}

\noindent\textbf{We make the following contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Two named failure modes in CVaR-constrained financial RL, with diagnostics
and, where demonstrated, fixes.} A \emph{Lagrangian constraint-coupling failure}: a scale
mismatch between the reward and constraint channels leaves the multiplier inert, through an
unstandardised cost advantage in the model-free actor--critic and through a dual step
mis-scaled to the CVaR excess in the differentiable allocator. The mismatch is specific to
portfolio allocation, where weekly returns are $O(10^{-3})$ and the constraint signal is
$O(10^{-2})$ to $O(1)$ (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}). An \emph{evaluation-protocol reversal}: on a 31-asset
universe a single stress window ranks the learned allocator above every optimiser, while a
rolling walk-forward inverts that ranking, so ``beat-the-optimiser'' results can be
protocol artefacts (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
\item \textbf{A differentiable CVaR-constrained allocator whose tail effect survives
multiplicity control.} On a leak-free, tested portfolio-control environment, a return
objective plus a differentiable CVaR penalty is back-propagated through the rollout, with a
Lagrangian dual on a breach-rate budget and a residual policy over an adaptive anchor
(Section~\ref{sec:method}). The tail-risk reduction holds on a 40-fold, two-universe
walk-forward under Benjamini--Hochberg and Deflated-Sharpe control
(Section~\ref{sec:significance}), and a regime-switching hybrid converts it into a
walk-forward Sharpe gain of $1.316$ vs $1.209$ for pure min-variance, with overlapping
bootstrap intervals (Sections~\ref{sec:results},~\ref{sec:discussion}).\looseness=-1
\item \textbf{An evaluation protocol built to expose exactly this kind of artefact.}
Deterministic optimisers plus three tuned model-free RL families are compared under a
two-universe rolling walk-forward with Wilcoxon, Benjamini--Hochberg, and Deflated-Sharpe
control. This design is what surfaces the protocol reversal in Contribution 1. The
regime-switching hybrid is a supporting observation within this protocol rather than a
separate contribution.
\end{enumerate}

The pure learned allocator does \textbf{not} beat a rolling minimum-variance optimiser in
aggregate, and we do not claim that it should. The \emph{constrained} allocator's value lies
in tail control, which the regime-switching hybrid converts into an out-of-sample Sharpe gain
($+0.107$) in the high-volatility regime where the constraint is most active. The broader
contribution is a differentiable constraint mechanism that demonstrably binds and cuts tails,
together with two transferable failure modes that any study of constrained RL in finance
must control for. The allocator is the testbed that exposes both.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Mean--variance optimisation and its inputs} Mean--variance optimisation
(MVO)~\cite{markowitz1952portfolio} is sensitive to input estimates. Decision-focused
learning trains return predictors to improve downstream MVO decisions rather than raw forecast
accuracy~\cite{lee2024dfl}, and mean--variance-efficient collaborative filtering
blends user preference with portfolio theory~\cite{chung2023mvecf}. Both improve the
\emph{inputs} to a variance-based objective. We instead constrain a \emph{tail} risk
measure on a learned policy.\looseness=-1

\paragraph{Conditional Value-at-Risk} $\mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha$ is the expected
portfolio loss on the worst $(1{-}\alpha)$ fraction of outcomes, the average of losses
exceeding the $\alpha$-quantile ($\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha$). Unlike variance, it ignores the
upside, and unlike $\mathrm{VaR}$, it averages across the whole tail. Rockafellar and
Uryasev~\cite{rockafellar2000optimization} give the tractable form
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha(L) \;=\; \min_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}\!\left\{
    \zeta + \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max(L-\zeta,\,0)\right]
  \right\},
\end{equation}
where $L$ denotes the portfolio loss and the inner expectation is over the empirical
loss distribution. The minimum is attained at $\zeta=\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha$, and the
representation admits both a convex linear program (LP), which we include as a baseline, and
a differentiable penalty via \texttt{topk}.\looseness=-1

\paragraph{RL for portfolio management}
The dominant pattern in the RL-for-portfolio literature is to train on a single historical
window, evaluate on one held-out test split, and compare against equal-weight or
buy-and-hold. EIIE~\cite{jiang2017deep} compares twelve classical strategies on one
$\approx$50-day crypto window, and SARL~\cite{ye2020sarl} reports higher return on a fixed
period without cross-validation. FinRL~\cite{liu2021finrl} supports rolling retraining but
demonstrates it on a single split with no significance tests. Huang~\cite{huang2025ctrl}
applies continuous-time RL to asset--liability management, although with a quadratic
surplus objective rather than a tail constraint. None of these uses CVaR as a constraint,
seeds multiple runs, or applies statistical testing. Empirical finance has long documented
how the selection of backtests inflates reported
performance~\cite{white2000reality,harvey2016cross,bailey2017pbo}, and our protocol carries
those controls over to RL portfolio allocation. We are not aware of prior work that subjects
RL portfolio allocation to a 40-fold, two-universe walk-forward with Wilcoxon and
Benjamini--Hochberg control, which is the protocol that exposes the reversal artefact.

\paragraph{Direct reinforcement and pathwise gradients}
Moody and Saffell~\cite{moody2001learning} first trained a recurrent trading policy by
back-propagating the \emph{differential Sharpe ratio} directly through the return
function. Buehler et al.~\cite{buehler2019deephedging} (Deep Hedging) extend this to
neural derivative hedging, back-propagating CVaR as a risk-aversion \emph{utility}
through a simulated trajectory. Our allocator inherits the pathwise-gradient insight
from both but replaces the risk-aversion coefficient with an explicit CVaR
\emph{budget constraint} enforced by a dual variable. Model-free policy gradients
(Advantage Actor-Critic, A2C, and Proximal Policy Optimization,
PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo}) and off-policy actor--critics
(Soft Actor-Critic, SAC~\cite{haarnoja2018soft}) ignore this differentiable structure. Our allocator
exploits it, which matters at this sample size (887 weekly observations,
Section~\ref{sec:modelfree}). ProtoHedge~\cite{faloughi2025protohedge} similarly makes
such policies auditable via a prototype-similarity policy.

\paragraph{CVaR-constrained RL}
Chow et al.~\cite{chow2018riskconstrained} derive the Lagrangian gradient for CVaR-constrained
CMDPs. Tessler et al.~\cite{tessler2019rcpo} (RCPO) and Ying et al.~\cite{ying2022cppo} (CPPO)
implement hard CVaR constraints for robotics control tasks. None identifies the
\emph{constraint-coupling failure} that we describe, wherein standardising the return
advantage to unit scale without normalising the cost advantage leaves the Lagrange
multiplier permanently inert. The failure is specific to portfolio allocation, where returns
are $O(10^{-3})$ and breach indicators are $O(1)$, and it would remain invisible in robotics.
We pose allocation as a constrained MDP~\cite{altman1999cmdp,achiam2017cpo} and show that a
well-scaled dual is what makes the constraint bind. The closest concurrent work is
\citet{dong2025clauselens} (\textsc{ClauseLens}), a CVaR-constrained RL system for
reinsurance pricing. Our contribution is orthogonal to it, in that we name failure modes that
transfer to any CVaR-constrained financial RL system.

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\paragraph{Markov Decision Process (MDP)} At weekly step $t$ the agent observes state $s_t$ (per-asset
26-week momentum and volatility, current weights, drawdown, a rolling 95\% CVaR
estimate, and optional exogenous macro/factor features) and chooses post-trade
weights $w_t$ on the long-only simplex. The environment projects $w_t$ onto the
admissible set $\mathcal{W}$ (max weight 0.40, turnover cap 0.50, optional cash
floor), charges proportional cost $c\lVert w_t - w_{t-1}\rVert_1$ ($c=5$\,bps), and
realises $r_t = w_t^{\top}\rho_t - c\lVert w_t - w_{t-1}\rVert_1$, where $\rho_t$
are next-week asset returns. Features at $t$ use only $\rho_{<t}$ (no look-ahead,
enforced by tests).\looseness=-1

\paragraph{Constrained objective (CMDP)} A \emph{constrained Markov Decision
Process} (CMDP)~\cite{altman1999cmdp} augments the standard MDP with one or more
inequality constraints that the policy must satisfy on average. We maximise
risk-adjusted return subject to a tail budget, $\max_\pi J(\pi)$ s.t.\
$\mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha(\text{loss})\le d$ with $\alpha=0.95$. The Lagrangian
relaxation converts the constraint into a penalty, $\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)=J(\pi) -
\lambda\,(\mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha - d)$, and a dual variable $\lambda\ge0$ is raised
whenever the constraint is violated and lowered when slack remains. In equilibrium,
$\lambda$ is the shadow price of tail risk: a higher $\lambda$ means that the policy
has been pushing against its tail budget.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Differentiable allocator} The actor is an MLP mapping $s_t$ to simplex
weights via softmax. Because the environment's reward and a sample-CVaR are
differentiable in $w$, we train by back-propagating, over randomised windows of
the training series, the objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = -\,\mathrm{Sharpe}(\text{net returns})
  \;+\; \lambda\,\mathrm{ReLU}(\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}} - d)
  \;+\; \kappa\,\text{turnover},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{Sharpe}=\bar{r}/\sigma_r$ is the annualised mean return divided by its
standard deviation (reward per unit of risk), $\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}$ is the mean
tail loss over the worst $(1{-}\alpha)$ fraction of window net returns (a differentiable
tail mean via \texttt{topk}), and $d$ is the pre-specified tail budget. For this
problem, the pathwise gradient is far more sample-efficient than score-function RL.

\paragraph{Pathwise gradient and where the constraint acts} Every term in
$\mathcal{L}$ is a differentiable function of the rollout, so the policy is trained
by exact back-propagation through the simplex map and the reward, without a
score-function estimator. With net returns $r_t = w_t^{\top}\rho_t - c\lVert w_t-w_{t-1}\rVert_1$
and tail set $\mathcal{T}=\{t: R_t \text{ among the worst } \lceil(1-\alpha)T\rceil
\text{ of the window}\}$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta}
= \sum_{t}\Big(
  \underbrace{-\tfrac{\partial\,\mathrm{Sharpe}}{\partial r_t}}_{\text{all weeks}}
  \;-\;
  \underbrace{\tfrac{\lambda}{|\mathcal{T}|}\,
  \mathbf{1}[\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}>d]\,\mathbf{1}[t\in\mathcal{T}]}_{\text{tail weeks only}}
  \Big)\,\frac{\partial r_t}{\partial w_t}\,\frac{\partial w_t}{\partial \theta},
\end{equation}
where $\partial w_t/\partial\theta$ flows through the residual softmax
(below). The \texttt{topk} subgradient routes the dual pressure to \emph{exactly}
the worst-$(1-\alpha)$ weeks, and only while the constraint is breached. The penalty
lifts the deep-loss weeks without distorting the bulk of the path. This locality is
what makes the constraint bind cleanly once the coupling is correctly scaled
(Section~\ref{sec:coupling}).

\paragraph{Residual policy over an adaptive anchor} To generalise across regimes
rather than overfit one price path, weights are $w_t = \mathrm{softmax}(\log a_t +
f_\theta(s_t))$, where $a_t$ is an adaptive inverse-volatility anchor computed from
recent returns. At initialisation $f_\theta \approx 0$, so the policy \emph{is} the
anchor (a strong, regime-robust prior), and training then learns a state-dependent tilt.
Model selection keeps the checkpoint with the best validation objective among those that
satisfy the validation CVaR limit, and weight decay is applied, both to curb backtest
overfitting.

\paragraph{Baselines} Deterministic, re-estimated weekly: cash, equal weight,
inverse vol, minimum variance, mean--variance, risk parity, and a min-CVaR
Rockafellar--Uryasev LP~\cite{rockafellar2000optimization}. The covariance-based rules use
the sample covariance of the trailing 104 weeks and inverse volatility the trailing 52 weeks,
all long-only under the learner's weight cap. Learned: model-free A2C,
PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} (clipped surrogate, generalised advantage estimation, GAE), SAC~\cite{haarnoja2018soft}
(off-policy, twin critics, auto-tuned entropy), and the unconstrained differentiable allocator ($\lambda=0$).

\paragraph{Regime-switching hybrid}
We construct a no-retrain hybrid: label each out-of-sample (OOS) week \emph{high-volatility} if
trailing 13-week SPY volatility meets the 75th-percentile cutoff (15\% of OOS weeks),
\emph{selloff} if cumulative return $\le -5\%$, else \emph{calm}. The hybrid applies
constrained RL in high-volatility weeks and rolling min-variance otherwise, reusing
the existing OOS paths without refitting.\looseness=-1

\emph{Threshold sensitivity.} Because the switch depends on two labelling thresholds,
we sweep them ($\text{vol-quantile}\in\{0.65,\dots,0.85\}$, $\text{selloff}
\in\{-0.04,-0.05,-0.06\}$, 15 configurations, no retraining, Table~\ref{tab:threshold}).
The hybrid beats pure min-variance in \textbf{10/15} configurations (mean gain
$+0.077$). It wins at every volatility cutoff when the selloff threshold is $-0.04$
or $-0.05$, but loses at all five cutoffs once the selloff threshold deepens to
$-0.06$, because that threshold reclassifies the high-volatility weeks in which the
constrained allocator adds most as selloffs and routes them to min-variance instead. The reported $+0.107$ at
the default $(0.75,-0.05)$ is representative rather than selected post hoc: the edge
holds for volatility-defined stress but not once the selloff label absorbs it.

\section{Decomposing the constraint: dual, architecture, and budget feasibility}
\label{sec:coupling}

We designed a three-arm diagnostic on the differentiable allocator to isolate what a
Lagrangian CVaR constraint contributes, and where it can fail silently. All three arms
share the identical pipeline (residual actor, CVaR penalty with dual ascent, CVaR-feasible
checkpoint selection on validation, admissible-set projection) and training budget
(5 seeds, 1500 updates). Only the dual's learning rate $\eta_\lambda$ and the
presence of the constraint differ (Table~\ref{tab:coupling}). The \textbf{mis-scaled
dual} ($\eta_\lambda=0.001$) takes steps far smaller than the CVaR excess it tracks
requires. The \textbf{scaled dual} ($\eta_\lambda=5.0$) matches the step to that scale.
The \textbf{unconstrained} arm removes the CVaR penalty and selects on validation
return alone.\looseness=-1

\paragraph{Finding 1: dual inertness from a scale mismatch} With the mis-scaled dual,
the multiplier stays near zero throughout training (mean final $\lambda=0.022$ vs.\
$0.101$ for the scaled dual). The dual ascends on the CVaR excess,
$\lambda \leftarrow \max\{0,\ \lambda + \eta_\lambda(\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}} - d)\}$,
which is $O(10^{-2})$ in weekly-return units, so a step of $10^{-3}$ cannot move
$\lambda$ within the training budget. The model-free actor--critic shows the same
failure through its advantages. With the reward advantage standardised and the cost
advantage left at its raw $O(10^{-3})$ scale, the constrained and unconstrained agents
trained identically until we standardised the cost advantage, after which $\lambda$ rose
to $O(1)$. The diagnostic transfers directly: multiplier telemetry that stays flat at zero
throughout training means that the constraint is decorative, whatever the loss curve shows.

\paragraph{Finding 2: most of the tail control is architectural, not the dual}
With $\lambda$ pinned near zero, the constrained pipeline alone (CVaR-feasible
checkpoint selection over the residual anchor, together with the projection) still cuts
CVaR$_{99}$ by $\mathbf{61\%}$ relative to the unconstrained learner ($0.025$ vs $0.065$,
5-seed mean) and raises Sharpe by $58\%$ ($0.987$ vs $0.626$). A permutation test on the
5-seed distributions confirms the difference (observed $-0.039$, one-sided $p=0.0044$,
two-sided $p=0.0083$, $n=50{,}000$). The scaled-dual arm produces a similar reduction
relative to the unconstrained learner ($p=0.0037$), so the gap does not depend on the dual
engaging. Engaging the dual correctly
($\lambda=O(0.1)$) leaves CVaR$_{99}$ essentially unchanged versus the near-inert
mis-scaled dual ($0.028$ vs $0.025$): most of the $61\%$ reduction is delivered by
the architecture, not the multiplier. This is a positive result, since a constrained
pipeline regularises the tail on its own, independently of the Lagrangian penalty. It is
also what makes the coupling failure silent: tail metrics improve with or without a working
dual, so only the multiplier telemetry reveals that the dual is not doing the work.

\paragraph{Finding 3: budget feasibility is a second knob, and it needs enforcement,
not another calibration} The scaled dual binds in training, but at the tight budget
($d=1.2\%$) produces a \emph{higher} OOS breach rate ($75\%$) than the mis-scaled dual
($8\%$), though classical rules clear the same budget in $92\%$ of weeks
(Section~\ref{sec:budget-reach}). The scaled dual is in fact worse than the mis-scaled
dual on every metric in Table~\ref{tab:coupling}, and model selection explains why.
Scaling the dual lets most checkpoints meet the validation budget (27 of 30 on average,
against 8 of 30), so selection keeps a later and more return-seeking checkpoint (update
1010 on average, against 370), and that checkpoint breaches under the test window's tail
shift. Four pre-specified rules for recalibrating $d$ from
validation data (a loose $1.92\%$ budget, matching the policy's own or the anchor's
validation-realised $\mathrm{CVaR}_{0.95}$, and a per-fold adaptive budget over a 10-fold
walk-forward) all land in a $71$--$78\%$ OOS breach band: a return-seeking Lagrangian
gives up compliance for return under tail shift whatever $d$ is, because the budget
itself was never the source of the failure.

\paragraph{The fix: enforce the budget rather than penalise its violation} We therefore
enforce the budget with a decision-time CVaR \emph{filter}. Between the actor's proposal and
the environment, we estimate forward
CVaR from a trailing covariance and mean only, both computed from returns strictly
before $t$ ($\mathrm{CVaR}\approx c_\alpha\sigma_p-\mu_p$, where $\mu_p$ and $\sigma_p$
are the trailing portfolio mean and volatility and
$c_\alpha=\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha))/(1-\alpha)$ is the Gaussian CVaR multiplier,
$c_{0.95}=2.06$). If the estimate exceeds $d$ we
shrink the proposal along the line toward a trailing minimum-variance anchor, solving
numerically for the largest mixing weight that clears the budget, then trade the
shrunk weights, with turnover/max-weight/long-only composed after as before. The
decision at $t$ depends only on the information set $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$: the trailing
mean, covariance and minimum-variance anchor use the $L=104$ returns
$\rho_{t-L},\dots,\rho_{t-1}$, and the actor's proposal uses features built from
$\rho_{<t}$. A look-ahead audit
(\texttt{tests/test\_risk\_filter\_no\_lookahead.py}) confirms the
decision at $t$ is unchanged by shifting the return series and is a pure function of
trailing statistics. Both checks pass with zero deviation. We train with the filter active.

Four arms mirror the coupling arms exactly (5 seeds, 1500 updates, identical splits),
changing only the named quantities (Table~\ref{tab:coupling}, bottom block,
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}). \textbf{A6, filter + scaled dual} (our intended method) cuts
breach from $75.1\%$ to $7.4\%$ against the scaled dual at the same $\eta_\lambda$, going
\emph{below} every classical rule, with $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ $0.016$ and mean excess
$0.0043$, at Sharpe $0.851$. \textbf{A5, filter only} ($\eta_\lambda=0.001$) coincides with
the mis-scaled row. That policy already sits within budget, so the filter never triggers
(0 of 1285 test weeks across seeds). It reaches the classical $7.9\%$ at Sharpe $0.987$
against minimum variance's $0.901$ and breaches less than half as deeply as minimum
variance (mean excess $0.0126$ vs.\ $0.0294$).
\textbf{A7, adaptive dual} ($\lambda$ updated online from realised past losses, filter
off) is the negative control: breach stays at $81.9\%$ even as $\lambda$ climbs to a
mean of $7.69$, so test-time dual adaptation alone enforces nothing, and the compliance
gain comes from the filter rather than from the adaptation. \textbf{A8} recovers most of the
compliance ($14.6\%$), although less reliably, ranging from $7$ to $33\%$ across seeds
against zero seed-to-seed variance for A5 and A6.\looseness=-1

The filtered arms sit in the low-volatility region. A5 has a mean weight-space $L_1$
distance of $0.476$ to rolling minimum variance, a correlation of $0.786$, and a different
top holding in $9.4\%$ of rebalances, against $0.890$, $0.201$ and $97.5\%$ for the
unfiltered scaled dual. The filtered policy is therefore min-variance-\emph{adjacent}
without being a redundant way to compute minimum variance. From that region it dominates
minimum variance on both axes, with higher Sharpe at an equal breach rate and half the
breach depth (A5), and a lower breach rate outright (A6). A soft Lagrangian cannot enforce a
tail budget out of sample, whereas a decision-time projection can.

\paragraph{Is the budget infeasible, or is the policy failing to reach it?}\label{sec:budget-reach}
When every classical baseline is re-scored against the identical
rolling-$\mathrm{CVaR}_{0.95}$ budget on the same realised path, the breach rate is
$7.9\%$ for minimum variance, inverse volatility and the min-CVaR LP, $62.8\%$ for
equal weight and $75.3\%$ for risk parity, against $75.1\%$ for the scaled-dual RL.
The budget is therefore reachable. The soft-penalty learner was not reaching it, and the
filter arms now match the classical rate from a learned policy.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The primary universe consists of seven liquid exchange-traded funds (ETFs) that span the
major macro blocks: SPY (equity), TLT (rates), HYG (credit), DBC (commodity), GLD (gold), UUP
(US dollar) and BIL (cash). Adjusted closes from Yahoo are resampled to \textbf{887 weekly
returns over 2008--2024}. Splits are
chronological (60/20/20): train $\approx$2008--2018 (incl.\ the 2008 global financial crisis, GFC), validation
$\approx$2018--2021, test $\approx$2021--2024. For the stress study we retrain on
2008--2018 and test on the 267-week window containing the 2020 COVID crash and the
2022 selloff. Macro covariates (10y$-$3m term spread, VIX) are sourced from index
proxies (\texttt{\^{}TNX$-$\^{}IRX}, \texttt{\^{}VIX}). A Federal Reserve Economic
Data (FRED) loader is also provided. Portfolios are long-only with a maximum weight of 0.40,
a turnover cap of 0.50 and gross exposure 1.0. Training uses 150 episodes and the seeds
$\{7,13,23,42,2025\}$, and significance tests use a 2000-sample block bootstrap (block 4),
Wilcoxon, BH and Deflated-Sharpe.

For the cross-universe significance test (Section~\ref{sec:significance}) we add a
second universe of ten S\&P sector ETFs (XLK, XLF, XLE, XLV, XLY, XLP, XLU, XLI, XLB,
plus a BIL cash leg), identical in every other respect (same provider, clock, window
and cost). The two universes therefore share a calendar, and
Section~\ref{sec:significance} clusters their folds accordingly. For the evaluation-protocol
experiment (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) we add a \textbf{third universe of 31
cross-asset, sector, and industry ETFs} (4 broad equity, 9 S\&P sectors, 7 industry,
6 fixed-income, 3 commodity, FX, cash) with the same provenance, clock and cost. Its
higher dimensionality stresses covariance estimation.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Sharpe ratios for the constrained allocator ($0.87$ to $0.99$) differ across tables because
configurations differ: 1,500 updates over 5 seeds for the coupling and filter arms, 2,500
over 5 seeds for the stress study, 2,000 over 3 seeds for the ablations, and a 3-seed
ensemble per walk-forward fold.\looseness=-1

\subsection{Calm chronological split: the constraint does not bind}
On the comparatively calm 2021--2024 test split, deterministic minimum-variance
(Sharpe 2.52, $\mathrm{CVaR}_{95}$ 0.0064) and the min-CVaR LP (2.24) dominate. The
differentiable allocator reaches Sharpe 1.24 (vs equal weight 0.82, inverse vol
1.45), and model-free A2C reaches only $\approx$0.79. No strategy breaches the CVaR
limit, so the constrained and unconstrained learners are approximately equal, and the
constraint costs almost nothing when there is no tail loss to prevent. The other deterministic rules trail on this split:
inverse volatility (Sharpe 1.45, $\mathrm{CVaR}_{95}$ 0.0087, max-DD 0.041), mean--variance
(0.84, 0.0198, 0.104), equal weight (0.82, 0.0163, 0.097) and risk parity (0.70, 0.0182,
0.134), while minimum variance and the min-CVaR LP share a max-DD of 0.016 and the LP has
$\mathrm{CVaR}_{95}$ 0.0080.\looseness=-1

\subsection{Stress window: the constraint binds}
Table~\ref{tab:stress} contrasts a return-greedy unconstrained learner with the
CVaR-constrained allocator on the stress window (mean $\pm$ std over 5 seeds). The
constraint roughly halves tail risk and drawdown while \emph{improving} Sharpe, Sortino
and Calmar, by removing the deep-loss weeks. The constrained policy rotates into the cash/rates
leg under stress, consistent with the lower drawdown and breach depth below.\looseness=-1

\subsection{Walk-forward across regimes (10 OOS folds, $\approx$2010--2024)}
With refits every 52 weeks on a rolling 312-week window and 10 concatenated
out-of-sample folds, the constraint beats unconstrained RL out of sample on every metric:
Sharpe $+18\%$ (0.70\,$\to$\,0.83), $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ $-20\%$ (0.0416\,$\to$\,0.0331)
and max-DD $-25\%$ (15.1\%\,$\to$\,11.4\%). Inverse volatility (Sharpe 1.06, max-DD 0.070,
$\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ 0.0183) and minimum variance (1.45, 0.057, 0.0106) remain ahead in
absolute terms. The fold-level Wilcoxon on $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$
is marginal on the macro universe alone ($p=0.06$, with 5 of 10 folds improved), and the
reduction concentrates in \textbf{selloffs} ($\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ $0.066\!\to\!0.048$,
max-DD $0.218\!\to\!0.137$, Table~\ref{tab:regime}). Minimum variance is therefore the
strongest absolute performer, a null result on ``beat the optimiser.'' A two-universe
40-fold analysis raises the significance to $p=8.8\times10^{-6}$
(Section~\ref{sec:significance}).

\paragraph{When RL adds value: regime-conditional breakdown}
\textbf{High-volatility} periods ($n{=}71$) are where the constrained policy performs
best relative to minimum variance: Sharpe $2.169$ versus $1.668$, with CVaR$_{99}$ within
$0.012$ of minimum variance's floor. In \textbf{calm} markets ($n{=}388$) and
\textbf{selloffs} ($n{=}61$) minimum variance dominates (selloff Sharpe $0.035$
vs.\ constrained RL $-0.809$): the overlay adds value when volatility is elevated
but has not yet collapsed into a full drawdown, and stops adding value once liquidity
deteriorates. The constraint still cuts tail risk relative to unconstrained RL in
\emph{all three} regimes ($-29.1\%$ calm, $-20.7\%$ high-vol, $-27.4\%$ selloff), which
points to a structural property of the architecture rather than a regime-specific
artefact.

\subsection{Statistical significance across two universes}
\label{sec:significance}
To confirm that the effect holds beyond a single asset universe, we add ten S\&P sector
ETFs (XLK, XLF, \dots, plus a cash leg), shorten the walk-forward test fold to 26~weeks to
obtain more observations (20 folds per universe), and pool the fold-level
$\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ differences (Table~\ref{tab:stats}). \textbf{Both universes individually show significant
fold-level reduction}: macro-ETF Wilcoxon $p=0.0077$ (9 folds improved, 11 tied, none
worse), sector Wilcoxon $p=0.0003$ (17 improved, 2 tied, 1 worse), and
Benjamini--Hochberg~\cite{benjamini1995fdr} rejects the null on \emph{both}
($\text{BH-reject}=\text{True}$). A tied fold is one where the budget never binds, so the
constrained and unconstrained learners coincide. Pooling all 40 folds gives Wilcoxon
$p=8.8\times10^{-6}$ and a bootstrap 95\,\% CI of $[-0.0089,\,-0.0041]$ on the mean fold
difference, and 26 of the 27 untied folds improve (sign test $p=4\times10^{-7}$). Because
the two universes share a calendar, we also average their differences within each
calendar fold. The resulting 20 clusters give Wilcoxon $p=3.3\times10^{-4}$ (exact
sign-flip $p=3.8\times10^{-5}$), and moving-block bootstrap intervals over consecutive
clusters exclude zero. The Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio~\cite{bailey2012sharpe} of the
constrained allocator is $\geq 0.99$ on both universes. The out-of-sample \emph{Sharpe
advantage over classical baselines}, however, does not survive a Deflated-Sharpe
correction~\cite{bailey2014deflated}, which discounts a Sharpe ratio's apparent
significance by the number of strategy configurations searched before reporting it. The
pooled-significant effect is on \emph{tail risk}, consistent with a safety layer rather
than an alpha source. Per-universe mean fold $\Delta\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ is $-0.0041$
(macro-ETF) and $-0.0087$ (sector).

\subsection{Generalisation to sector ETFs}
\label{sec:generalize}
Re-running the stress study unchanged on the ten-sector universe (mean over 3 seeds)
replicates the main effect. The constraint cuts $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ by $30\%$
($0.109\!\to\!0.076$), $\mathrm{CVaR}_{95}$ by $35\%$ ($0.058\!\to\!0.037$), maximum drawdown by $30\%$
($0.295\!\to\!0.208$), and the breach rate by $47\%$ ($0.75\!\to\!0.40$), at
\emph{no} Sharpe cost ($0.752\!\to\!0.748$). A paired block bootstrap of the
$\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ difference gives $-0.044$, with 95\% BCa interval
$[-0.050,\,-0.032]$ and $p\approx0$. The one difference from the macro universe is that
there the constraint \emph{improved} Sharpe (the avoided tail dominated), whereas on
sectors it is Sharpe-neutral. The effect that replicates is the tail and drawdown
reduction rather than a return gain.\looseness=-1

\subsection{Robustness and ablations}
The $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ reduction holds in \textbf{7/7} perturbations, even at
$3\times$ costs ($\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ 54\% lower, Sharpe 63\% above unconstrained).
Ablations (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) reveal a \textbf{monotone pattern}
(Figure~\ref{fig:hero}, right): tightening the tail budget from loose
($\alpha=0.95$, limit $1.92\%$) through base ($1.2\%$) to tight ($\alpha=0.99$ or
$0.72\%$) \emph{simultaneously} improves CVaR$_{99}$ and Sharpe at every step. The
tightest configuration reaches Sharpe~$1.27$ and CVaR$_{99}=1.4\%$, which is competitive
with rolling min-variance (Sharpe~1.45) without relaxing the guarantee. Tighter risk limits
commonly hurt return, whereas here they help, because the constraint's gradient pressure
acts only on deep-loss weeks and so removes the largest losses while barely touching the
rest of the return distribution.\looseness=-1

\paragraph{Features} Rolling factor betas alone slightly hurt performance. Adding macro state
gives Sharpe $+0.02$ (3-seed mean) but worsens CVaR$_{99}$ ($0.029\!\to\!0.031$).

\paragraph{Diagnostics: behaviour rather than metric gaming}
Three diagnostics confirm that the constraint changes \emph{behaviour} rather than gaming
the tail metric: the unconstrained allocator concentrates and holds through the 2020/2022
drawdowns, the constrained one rotates into the cash/rates leg and holds a lower,
steadier rolling CVaR estimate, and the tail control is paid for with modestly
higher turnover, not leverage or a single-asset bet. The policy stays long-only,
diversified, and within the turnover cap.

\subsection{Tuned model-free RL remains weak}
\label{sec:modelfree}
We sweep 36 PPO and 8 SAC configurations, select each by validation Sharpe, and
evaluate on the stress window (Table~\ref{tab:modelfree}). Tuning does not rescue
either family. The selected PPO overfits validation (Sharpe $1.70$) and then posts the
\emph{worst} OOS tail (Sharpe $0.67$, $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}=0.070$), while SAC collapses to
near-zero turnover (Sharpe $0.39$). Both are \emph{generalisation} failures: with 887
weekly observations, score-function and off-policy RL have too few effective samples. The constrained allocator matches minimum variance on Sharpe ($0.91$ vs
$0.90$) while reducing $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ by $27\%$ ($0.032$ vs $0.044$).

\subsection{Evaluation-protocol reversal}
\label{sec:protocol}
To probe how far the choice of \emph{evaluation protocol} alone can move conclusions,
we add a third, high-dimensional universe, \textbf{31 cross-asset, sector, and
industry ETFs} (same provenance, clock, costs), where covariance estimation is
harder and a learner has, a priori, more scope to add value. On a single stress split
(train pre-2018, test through 2024) the constrained learner appears to \emph{beat
every optimiser}: Sharpe $0.85$ versus minimum variance $0.23$
(Table~\ref{tab:protocol}). The same minimum-variance optimiser is instead the
\emph{best} method by a wide margin under a rolling walk-forward (20 OOS folds,
refit every 26 weeks): Sharpe $\mathbf{1.40}$ versus the learner's $0.74$, so the
ranking \textbf{inverts}. The stress-split ``win'' was
an artefact of one fixed period, and rolling refits remove it entirely. It is not an
artefact of an unshrunk covariance on 31 assets: on the same split, Ledoit--Wolf
minimum variance~\cite{ledoit2004wellconditioned} reaches Sharpe $0.25$ and the min-CVaR
LP $0.26$. The constraint's tail-risk effect survives both
protocols (it still cuts $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ versus the unconstrained learner
fold-by-fold, Wilcoxon $p=6\times10^{-4}$), but the apparent
\emph{beat-the-optimiser} result does not. We treat rolling walk-forward with
Deflated-Sharpe~\cite{bailey2014deflated} as the reporting standard, and flag single-stress-window
comparisons of learned allocators against optimisers as unreliable.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Is the learner just minimum variance under another name?} The answer differs by
arm. The \emph{unfiltered} learner is far from it: its mean per-rebalance $L_1$ weight
distance to the rolling min-variance path is $0.89$ (of a maximum $2.0$), with correlation
$0.20$ and a different top-weighted asset in $97\%$ of weeks, so the resemblance that the
tightest ablation suggests (Figure~\ref{fig:hero}, right) lies in \emph{aggregate risk
statistics} rather than holdings. The filtered arms sit closer ($L_1$ $0.48$ to $0.61$,
correlation $0.72$ to $0.79$), buying compliance partly by occupying the low-volatility
region, and from there dominate minimum variance on Sharpe and breach depth
(Section~\ref{sec:coupling}).

For practitioners, (i)~tail limits de-risk \emph{proactively}, as the CVaR constraint
shifts to cash two weeks before the COVID trough (max drawdown $11.8\%$ vs $20.6\%$
unconstrained), (ii)~the constraint is best used as a \emph{high-volatility overlay}
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), and (iii)~a near-zero multiplier signals an inert constraint, so
coupling should be verified, and evaluation should span out-of-sample folds before an
optimiser edge is claimed.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:limitations}
Our claims are deliberately scoped: the multiplicity-controlled effect is tail-risk
reduction rather than alpha. The pure learned allocator does not beat rolling
minimum variance on Sharpe out of sample, and a Deflated-Sharpe correction removes its
apparent Sharpe edge over classical baselines, so the hybrid's $+0.107$ gain is
regime-confined. The study covers two equity-centric universes at weekly frequency with
proportional costs (no market impact or borrow), macro features are availability-lagged
but not vintage, and the protocol reversal is shown on a single 31-asset window. The
filter's Gaussian CVaR estimate can understate fat-tailed losses, and the constrained
learner trails minimum variance in selloff weeks (Table~\ref{tab:regime}). These
limitations bound the claims to a tail-risk safety layer and transferable evaluation
lessons rather than a state-of-the-art allocator.\looseness=-1

\section{Conclusion and future work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We present a differentiable CVaR-constrained RL allocator and show that (i)~the constraint
reliably reduces tail risk (26 of 27 untied folds, $p=8.8\!\times\!10^{-6}$) and eliminates
portfolio-constraint violations in stress, (ii)~a regime-switching hybrid reaches Sharpe
$1.316$ vs $1.209$ for min-variance, with overlapping intervals, and (iii)~the constraint
de-risks \emph{before} crisis troughs. Two failure modes transfer to any constrained
financial RL study: a \textbf{Lagrangian constraint-coupling failure} and an
\textbf{evaluation-protocol reversal}. A decision-time CVaR filter delivers out-of-sample
budget compliance, which neither rescaling the dual nor recalibrating the budget could.
Constrained RL is best positioned as a \emph{safety layer and high-volatility
overlay}: the effects that survive are tail-risk control and budget compliance, not alpha.\looseness=-1
