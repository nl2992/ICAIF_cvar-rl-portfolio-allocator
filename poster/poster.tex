\block{Abstract}{\body
  A portfolio allocator trained by reinforcement learning (RL) to maximise return has no
  incentive to limit large losses. The usual safeguard caps Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR),
  the mean loss in the worst weeks, through a Lagrangian penalty. We identify two ways in which
  this safeguard fails without visible symptoms. When the penalty and the reward are on
  different scales, the multiplier stays near zero and the budget is never enforced, while tail
  metrics still improve through other parts of the pipeline. The evaluation protocol can also
  decide the ranking. On a 31-asset universe, a single stress window placed the learned
  allocator above every classical optimiser, and a rolling walk-forward reversed the order. We
  add a decision-time filter that forecasts the CVaR of each proposed portfolio from trailing
  data and shrinks it toward minimum variance when the forecast exceeds the budget. At matched
  settings the filter lowered the out-of-sample breach rate from \hl{75.1\% to 7.4\%}. Over a
  40-fold walk-forward on two ETF universes, the constraint reduced $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ in
  \hl{26 of 27} untied folds (Wilcoxon $p=8.8\times10^{-6}$), and the result held after
  Benjamini-Hochberg correction and calendar clustering.
}

\block{1\quad Introduction}{\body
  In the week of 16 March 2020 the S\&P 500 fell about 15\% over five sessions and the VIX
  closed at 82.69. An allocator trained without a loss limit continued to hold equities. An
  otherwise identical allocator with a CVaR budget had moved into Treasuries and cash before
  the trough, and its drawdown was 7.7\% against 16.3\% (Fig.~1). Over 2020 to 2024 the
  maximum drawdowns were 11.8\% and 20.6\%.
  \fig[0.58\linewidth]{figure_stress_paths.pdf}
  \figcap{Figure 1. Drawdown on the 2020 to 2024 stress window. Thick lines follow the
  five-seed mean return path and thin lines the individual seeds.}
  The standard way to impose such a limit in RL is a Lagrangian penalty [2] whose multiplier
  $\lambda$ rises while the budget is exceeded. Safe-RL work has reported that $\lambda$
  responds slowly and depends on its step size [3], and OmniSafe standardises the cost signal
  by default [4]. Portfolio RL studies usually report a single test window, although
  optimised portfolios that look strong in one sample often fail out of sample [6,\,7]. The
  poster makes three contributions.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
    \item Two failure modes with diagnostics, a Lagrangian constraint-coupling failure and an
      evaluation-protocol reversal.
    \item A differentiable CVaR-constrained allocator with a decision-time filter that reduced
      out-of-sample breach from 75.1\% to 7.4\% at matched dual settings.
    \item A two-universe rolling walk-forward with Wilcoxon, Benjamini-Hochberg and Deflated
      Sharpe controls.
  \end{enumerate}
}

\block{2\quad Problem formulation and data}{\body
  Each week $t$ the agent observes $s_t$ (26-week momentum and volatility per asset, current
  weights, drawdown and a rolling 95\% CVaR estimate) and chooses post-trade weights $w_t$ on
  the long-only simplex. The environment projects $w_t$ onto the admissible set (maximum
  weight 0.40, turnover cap 0.50) and realises $r_t = w_t^{\top}\rho_t - c\lVert w_t -
  w_{t-1}\rVert_1$ with $c=5$\,bps. Features at $t$ use only returns before $t$. The problem
  is a constrained MDP [2],
  \[
    \max_\pi J(\pi)\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(L)\le d,\qquad
    \mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha(L)=\min_{\zeta}\Big\{\zeta+\tfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\,
      \mathbb{E}\big[(L-\zeta)^{+}\big]\Big\}\ [1],
  \]
  with $\alpha=0.95$ and $d=1.2\%$. A portfolio is over budget when its
  $\mathrm{CVaR}_{0.95}$ over the trailing 52 realised weeks exceeds $d$. The breach rate is
  the share of test weeks over budget, with 5\% tolerated. Most results report
  $\mathrm{CVaR}_{0.99}$, a deeper tail than the one constrained.

  The primary universe has seven ETFs (SPY, TLT, HYG, DBC, GLD, UUP, BIL) with 887 weekly
  returns from 2008 to 2024. The stress study trains on 2008 to 2018 and tests on the 267
  weeks that contain the 2020 crash and the 2022 selloff. A ten-sector ETF universe and a
  31-asset cross-asset universe use the same source, clock and costs. Seeds are 7, 13, 23, 42
  and 2025.
}

\block{3\quad Method}{\body
  The actor is an MLP whose output tilts an adaptive inverse-volatility anchor $a_t$, so
  that $w_t=\mathrm{softmax}(\log a_t+f_\theta(s_t))$ starts at the anchor. Over randomised
  training windows we back-propagate
  \[
    \mathcal{L}=-\mathrm{Sharpe}+\lambda\,\mathrm{ReLU}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}-d\big)
      +\kappa\,\mathrm{turnover},\qquad
    \lambda\leftarrow\max\{0,\ \lambda+\eta_\lambda(\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}-d)\},
  \]
  where $\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}$ is the mean loss over the worst $(1-\alpha)$ fraction of
  window returns. Its \texttt{topk} subgradient acts only on tail weeks. The selected
  checkpoint has the best validation objective among those within the validation CVaR limit.

  Before each trade, the filter forecasts the proposal's CVaR from the trailing $L=104$
  weekly returns as $\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}(w)=c_\alpha\sigma_p-\mu_p$, with
  $c_\alpha=\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha))/(1-\alpha)$ and $c_{0.95}=2.06$. If the forecast
  exceeds $d$ it trades
  \[
    w(s^\ast)=s^\ast w+(1-s^\ast)\,w^{\mathrm{MV}},\qquad
    s^\ast=\max\{s\in[0,1]:\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}(w(s))\le d\},
  \]
  where $w^{\mathrm{MV}}$ is the trailing minimum-variance portfolio, following the
  safety-layer idea of [5]. The decision at $t$ depends only on $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$, and a
  shifted-returns test showed zero deviation.
  \par\smallskip\centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      box/.style={draw=dark, line width=1mm, rounded corners=2mm, align=center,
        text width=96mm, minimum height=40mm, inner sep=3mm, font=\fontsize{20}{24}\selectfont,
        fill=white},
      ours/.style={box, draw=navy, fill=tint, line width=1.6mm},
      arr/.style={-{Latex[length=7mm, width=7mm]}, line width=1.3mm, color=dark},
      node distance=14mm and 14mm]
    \node[box] (s) {\textbf{State $s_t$}\\ momentum, volatility, weights, drawdown, CVaR};
    \node[box, right=of s] (a) {\textbf{Residual actor}\\
      $\mathrm{softmax}(\log a_t+f_\theta(s_t))$};
    \node[ours, right=of a] (f) {\textbf{\color{navy}CVaR filter}\\
      shrink toward $w^{\mathrm{MV}}$ if $\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}>d$};
    \node[box, below=of f] (p) {\textbf{Projection}\\ weight and turnover caps, long-only};
    \node[box, below=of a] (m) {\textbf{Market}\\ net return $r_t$};
    \draw[arr] (s) -- (a);
    \draw[arr] (a) -- (f);
    \draw[arr] (f) -- (p);
    \draw[arr] (p) -- (m);
    \draw[arr, color=mid, dashed] (m.north) -- node[left, font=\fontsize{18}{21}\selectfont,

  \end{tikzpicture}
  \par\figcap{Figure 2. Allocation pipeline. The filter sits between the actor and the
  projection.}
}

\block{4\quad Evaluation-protocol reversal}{\body
  On the 31-asset universe, a single stress split (training before 2018, testing through 2024)
  placed the constrained learner above every optimiser, with Sharpe 0.85 against 0.23 for
  minimum variance. Under a rolling walk-forward with 20 out-of-sample folds refit every 26
  weeks, minimum variance led with 1.40 against 0.74 (Fig.~3). Covariance shrinkage did not
  account for the split result, since Ledoit-Wolf minimum variance [8] reached 0.25 and the
  min-CVaR LP 0.26 on the same split. The tail-risk reduction against unconstrained RL held
  under both protocols (fold-level Wilcoxon $p=6\times10^{-4}$), whereas the Sharpe advantage
  over the optimiser appeared only in the single split.
  \fig[0.5\linewidth]{figure_protocol.pdf}
  \figcap{Figure 3. Sharpe ratio of four methods under two protocols, 31-asset universe.}
  \keynote{Comparisons between learned allocators and optimisers should use rolling
  walk-forward folds with Deflated Sharpe [7]. Single stress-window comparisons are unreliable
  for this purpose.}
}

\block{5\quad Constraint-coupling failure}{\body
  Four arms of the differentiable allocator share the actor, projection and budget (5 seeds,
  1500 updates). They are A1 unconstrained, A2 mis-scaled dual ($\eta_\lambda=0.001$), A3
  scaled dual ($\eta_\lambda=5.0$) and A4 scaled dual with a loose budget ($d=1.92\%$). With
  the mis-scaled dual, $\lambda$ stayed near zero for the whole run, with a mean final value of
  0.022 against 0.101 for the scaled dual (Fig.~4). The CVaR excess is of order $10^{-2}$ in
  weekly-return units, so a step of $10^{-3}$ moves $\lambda$ by about $10^{-5}$ per update.
  A model-free actor-critic failed in the same way. Its reward advantage was standardised
  while its cost advantage stayed near $10^{-3}$, and the constrained and unconstrained agents
  trained identically until the cost advantage was also standardised.
  \fig[0.76\linewidth]{figure_lambda_trace.pdf}
  \figcap{Figure 4. The Lagrange multiplier over training. Thick lines are five-seed means.}
  Tail metrics do not reveal the failure. Even with $\lambda$ near zero, the constrained
  pipeline lowered $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ by 61\% relative to A1 (0.025 against 0.065) and
  raised Sharpe from 0.626 to 0.987 (permutation test, one-sided $p=0.0044$,
  $n=50{,}000$). The reduction came from checkpoint selection under the CVaR limit together
  with the shared anchor and projection. Engaging the dual changed $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$
  little (0.028 against 0.025).
  \keynote{A multiplier that remains at zero throughout training means the constraint has no
  effect, whatever the loss curve shows.}
}

\block{6\quad Decision-time CVaR filter}{\body
  Scaling the dual made the penalty bind in training but did not keep the portfolio within
  budget out of sample. A3 breached in 75.1\% of test weeks, against 7.9\% for A2. With the
  dual scaled, 27 of 30 checkpoints met the validation budget (8 of 30 for A2), so selection
  kept later and more return-seeking checkpoints (update 1010 on average, against 370). Four
  pre-specified rules for setting $d$ from validation data all gave 71 to 78\% breach.
  Classical rules scored against the same rolling budget reached 7.9\% (minimum variance,
  inverse volatility and the min-CVaR LP), so the budget was feasible. Arms A5 to A8 repeat
  the setup and change only the named components (Table 1, Fig.~5).

    the 5\% tolerance.}

  triggered (0 of 1285 test weeks) because the mis-scaled policy was already within budget.
  A7 updated $\lambda$ online from realised losses without the filter and still breached in
  81.9\% of weeks as $\lambda$ rose to 7.69, which attributes the improvement in A6 to the
  filter. A8 reached 14.6\%, with a range of 7 to 33\% across seeds. The filtered weights
  stayed measurably different from minimum variance (A5 mean $L_1$ distance 0.476,
  correlation 0.786).
}

\block{8\quad Regimes and baselines}{\body
  In the 10-fold macro walk-forward, the constrained policy did best relative to minimum
  variance in high-volatility weeks ($n=71$, Sharpe 2.169 against 1.668) and worst in selloffs
  ($n=61$, $-0.809$ against 0.035). Its $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ was below the unconstrained
  learner's in every regime ($-29.1\%$ calm, $-20.7\%$ high volatility, $-27.4\%$ selloff). A
  hybrid that applies constrained RL in high-volatility weeks and minimum variance otherwise
  reached Sharpe 1.316 (95\% CI $[0.66,\,2.03]$) against 1.209 ($[0.55,\,1.93]$), and the
  intervals overlap. It beat minimum variance in 10 of 15 threshold settings. Tuned PPO (36
  configurations) and SAC (8) did not generalise on 887 weekly observations. The selected PPO
  reached Sharpe 0.67 with $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ 0.070 on the stress window, and SAC 0.39.
}

\block{9\quad Limitations and conclusion}{\body
  The learned allocator did not beat rolling minimum variance on Sharpe out of sample, and the
  hybrid's gain came only from high-volatility weeks. The study uses weekly data with
  proportional costs and no market impact, the protocol reversal was shown on one 31-asset
  window, and the Gaussian forecast in the filter can understate fat-tailed losses. Within
  these limits, the constraint reduced tail risk in a way that survived multiple-testing
  correction, and the decision-time filter achieved the out-of-sample compliance that neither
  rescaling the dual nor recalibrating the budget achieved. Practitioners can detect the first
  failure by plotting $\lambda$ during training and the second by evaluating on rolling folds.
