\block{Abstract}{\body
  Reinforcement learning (RL) portfolio allocators are often given a tail-risk limit through a
  Lagrangian penalty on Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). We show two ways in which this limit
  can fail with no visible symptom. First, when the penalty and the reward are on different
  scales, the penalty weight $\lambda$ never moves away from zero, yet tail metrics still
  improve because of other parts of the pipeline. Second, the choice of test decides how the
  learner ranks against classical optimisers. We add a filter that checks each portfolio before
  it trades and moves it toward minimum variance when its forecast CVaR exceeds the budget. Out
  of sample, the share of weeks over budget fell from \hl{75.1\% to 7.4\%}. Across 40
  walk-forward folds on two ETF universes, the constraint lowered $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ in
  \hl{26 of the 27} folds where it was active ($p=8.8\times10^{-6}$).
}

\block{1\quad Motivation}{\body
  In the week of 16 March 2020 the S\&P 500 fell about 15\% and the VIX closed at 82.69. An RL
  allocator trained only for return kept its equities. The same allocator trained with a CVaR
  budget had already moved into Treasuries and cash, and its drawdown was 7.7\% against
  16.3\% (Fig.~1).
  \fig[0.68\linewidth]{figure_stress_paths.pdf}
  \figcap{Figure 1. Drawdown over 2020 to 2024. Thick lines are five-seed means and thin lines
  individual seeds.}
  A budget of this kind is usually imposed with a Lagrangian penalty whose weight $\lambda$
  grows while the portfolio is over budget [2]. Section 4 asks whether $\lambda$ actually
  grows, and Section 5 asks whether the test used to judge the result can be trusted.
}

\block{2\quad Tail risk and the budget}{\body
  $\mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha$ is the average loss in the worst $(1-\alpha)$ share of weeks [1].
  With $\alpha=0.95$ it averages the worst 5\%. It looks only at losses, and it measures how
  large they are beyond the Value-at-Risk (VaR) threshold (Fig.~2).
  \fig[0.72\linewidth]{figure_cvar_tail.pdf}
  \figcap{Figure 2. Weekly losses of the two allocators on the 2020 to 2024 stress window.
  CVaR is the mean of the losses to the right of VaR.}
  Each week the allocator chooses long-only weights $w_t$ on seven ETFs (SPY, TLT, HYG, DBC,
  GLD, UUP, BIL), with at most 40\% in one asset, weekly turnover at most 0.50 and a 5\,bps
  cost. The problem is a constrained Markov decision process [2],
  \[
    \max_\pi\ J(\pi)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathrm{CVaR}_{0.95}(L)\le d=1.2\%,\qquad
    \mathrm{CVaR}_\alpha(L)=\min_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}\Big\{\zeta+\tfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\,
      \mathbb{E}\big[(L-\zeta)^{+}\big]\Big\}.
  \]
  The data are 887 weekly returns from 2008 to 2024. Models train on 2008 to 2018 and are
  tested on the 267 weeks that include the 2020 crash and the 2022 selloff. A test week is a
  breach when the trailing 52-week $\mathrm{CVaR}_{0.95}$ exceeds $d$, and up to 5\% of weeks
  may breach.
}

\block{3\quad Method}{\body
  The allocator is trained end to end through the portfolio return, and a filter checks
  every portfolio before it trades (Fig.~3).
  \par\medskip\centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      v/.style={font=\fontsize{30}{34}\selectfont, inner sep=2mm},
      def/.style={font=\fontsize{18.5}{22}\selectfont, text=dark, align=center, text width=74mm},
      map/.style={-{Latex[length=6mm, width=6mm]}, line width=1.2mm, color=dark},
      lab/.style={above, font=\fontsize{21}{24}\selectfont, text=ink},
      node distance=0mm]
    \node[v] (s) at (0,0) {$s_t$};
    \node[v] (wt) at (7.6,0) {$\tilde w_t$};
    \node[v] (wh) at (15.6,0) {$\hat w_t$};
    \node[v] (w) at (23.4,0) {$w_t$};
    \node[v] (r) at (30.6,0) {$r_t$};
    \draw[map] (s) -- node[lab] {$f_\theta$} (wt);
    \draw[map, color=navy, line width=1.8mm] (wt) -- node[lab, text=navy] {filter $\Phi_d$} (wh);
    \draw[map] (wh) -- node[lab] {$\Pi_{\mathcal W}$} (w);
    \draw[map] (w) -- node[lab] {market} (r);
    \node[def, below=5mm of s] {state: momentum,\\ volatility, weights,\\ drawdown};
    \node[def, below=5mm of wt] {$\mathrm{softmax}(\log a_t+f_\theta(s_t))$,\\ $a_t$ inverse-vol anchor};
    \node[def, below=5mm of wh, text=navy] {$s^\ast\tilde w_t+(1-s^\ast)\,w^{\mathrm{MV}}_t$};
    \node[def, below=5mm of w] {$w\ge0,\ w_i\le0.4$,\\ $\lVert w-w_{t-1}\rVert_1\le0.5$};
    \node[def, below=5mm of r] {$w_t^{\top}\rho_t-c\lVert w_t-w_{t-1}\rVert_1$};
    \draw[map, dashed, color=mid] (r.north) -- ++(0,26mm) -| node[pos=0.25, above,
      font=\fontsize{19}{22}\selectfont, text=dark]
      {training: $\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(\theta,\lambda)$ through the rollout} ($(s)!0.5!(wt)+(0,14mm)$);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \par\figcap{Figure 3. The decision at week $t$ as a composition of maps. The navy map is the
  decision-time filter.}
  \RaggedRight
  Training minimises, over random windows of past weeks,
  \[
    \mathcal{L}(\theta,\lambda)=-\mathrm{Sharpe}(r)+\lambda\,\mathrm{ReLU}
      \big(\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}-d\big)+\kappa\,\mathrm{turnover},\qquad
    \lambda\leftarrow\max\{0,\ \lambda+\eta_\lambda(\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}-d)\},
  \]
  where $\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}$ is the mean of the worst 5\% of returns in the window, so its
  gradient acts only on those weeks. The filter, a safety layer in the sense of [3], forecasts
  CVaR from the last 104 weeks as $\widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}(w)=c_\alpha\sigma_p(w)-\mu_p(w)$
  with $c_{0.95}=2.06$ (Gaussian). An over-budget proposal is mixed with the trailing
  minimum-variance portfolio, keeping as much of the proposal as the budget allows,
  \[
    s^\ast=\max\big\{s\in[0,1]:\ \widehat{\mathrm{CVaR}}\big(s\,\tilde w_t+(1-s)\,
      w^{\mathrm{MV}}_t\big)\le d\big\}.
  \]
  \fig[0.98\linewidth]{figure_filter_shrink.pdf}
  \figcap{Figure 4. The filter on 16 March 2020 for an illustrative equity-heavy proposal,
  using the trailing ETF data. (a) Forecast CVaR along the mixing line. The budget is met at
  $s^\ast=0.34$. (b) Weights before and after.}
  The filter uses only data up to the previous week, and an automated look-ahead test
  confirms this.
}

\block{4\quad Failure 1. The penalty weight never moves}{\body
  With a dual step $\eta_\lambda=10^{-3}$, $\lambda$ stayed near zero for all 1500 training
  updates (Fig.~5). The CVaR excess is about $10^{-2}$ per week, so each update moved
  $\lambda$ by about $10^{-5}$. With $\eta_\lambda=5.0$, $\lambda$ rose to between 0.1 and
  0.3. A model-free actor-critic failed in the same way when its cost signal (about
  $10^{-3}$) was left unstandardised next to a unit-scale reward signal.
  \fig[0.76\linewidth]{figure_lambda_trace.pdf}
  \figcap{Figure 5. The penalty weight $\lambda$ during training, five seeds.}
  Tail metrics do not reveal the failure. Even with $\lambda$ near zero, the rest of the
  pipeline (the anchor, the projection and checkpoint selection under the CVaR limit) lowered
  $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ by 61\% against the unconstrained learner (0.025 against 0.065,
  permutation test $p=0.0044$).
  \keynote{Plot $\lambda$ during training. A multiplier that stays at zero means the
  constraint is inactive.}
}

\block{5\quad Failure 2. The test decides the ranking}{\body
  A walk-forward test retrains the model on a rolling window and evaluates it on the next
  unseen block, repeatedly. On a 31-asset universe, one fixed stress split ranked the
  constrained learner above minimum variance (Sharpe 0.85 against 0.23). A 20-fold
  walk-forward reversed the order (0.74 against 1.40, Fig.~6). Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage [5] did
  not change the split result (0.25). The tail-risk reduction against unconstrained RL held
  under both tests (Wilcoxon $p=6\times10^{-4}$).
  \fig[0.58\linewidth]{figure_protocol.pdf}
  \figcap{Figure 6. The same four methods ranked by two tests, 31-asset universe.}
  \keynote{Compare learners with optimisers on rolling walk-forward folds and report the
  Deflated Sharpe ratio [4].}
}

\block{6\quad The filter keeps the portfolio within budget}{\body
  A larger dual step made $\lambda$ move, but the portfolio was then over budget in 75.1\% of
  test weeks. Classical rules met the same budget in 92\% of weeks, so the budget was
  attainable. Adding the filter lowered the breach rate to \hl{7.4\%}, with
  $\mathrm{CVaR}_{99}$ of 0.016 and Sharpe 0.851 (Fig.~7). A control that raised $\lambda$
  during testing without the filter stayed at 81.9\%, so the improvement comes from the
  filter.
  \fig[0.72\linewidth]{figure_filter_frontier.pdf}
  \figcap{Figure 7. Share of weeks over budget against Sharpe on the stress window. The
  dashed line is the 5\% tolerance.}
}

\block{8\quad Conclusion}{\body
  The constraint lowered tail risk in a way that survived multiple-testing correction, and
  the filter achieved the out-of-sample compliance that rescaling the dual did not. The
  learner did not beat rolling minimum variance on Sharpe out of sample. The study uses weekly
  data with proportional costs, and the Gaussian forecast can understate fat tails.
